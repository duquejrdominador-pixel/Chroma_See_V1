Why ChromaSee existsRationale

Colour blindness is common, largely undiagnosed, and rarely accommodated.

Color vision deficiency (CVD), commonly known as color blindness, is among the most widespread inherited conditions in the world and among the least accommodated. A systematic review and meta-analysis drawing on more than 1.7 million participants across 56 studies and 21 countries placed the global prevalence of congenital CVD at 2.59 percent among children and adolescents, rising to 4.38 percent among males. In populations of Northern European descent, red–green deficiency reaches approximately 8 percent of males and 0.5 percent of females. Taken together, prevailing estimates place the number of affected individuals worldwide at roughly 300 million. The condition is not a marginal case; it describes a population larger than that of most nations on earth, distributed across every region and every income level.

Prevalence, however, is not uniform. Rates reported in Asian and African populations are generally lower than those observed in European ones, with figures for Chinese males ranging from 4.0 to 6.9 percent and Japanese males near 4.0 percent. Philippine data fall within this range. A screening of 1,258 male students in a public high school identified color-vision deficiency in 5.17 percent of participants, of whom 98 percent were classified as deutan, the subtype associated with the medium-wavelength cone. Applied to national population figures, a rate of this order implies that several million Filipinos live with some degree of CVD. The condition is therefore neither rare nor foreign to the setting in which this study was conducted.

Fewer than one in five students found to be colour deficient had known of it beforehand.

What makes these figures consequential is how little of the affected population is ever identified. CVD is congenital in the overwhelming majority of cases, yet diagnosis is routinely delayed until school age or later because color-vision screening is not standard practice in many health and education systems. Awareness among those affected is correspondingly poor; in one school-based study, fewer than one in five students found to be color deficient had known of their condition beforehand. Where screening is not mandated, individuals commonly discover the deficiency only after repeated failure at color-dependent tasks, or at the point where an occupational requirement excludes them from a field they have already trained for.

The costs of that invisibility accumulate quietly. In the classroom, color-coded charts, maps, diagrams, and laboratory materials place undiagnosed learners at a measurable disadvantage, and their mistakes are frequently misread as inattention or carelessness rather than as a sensory limitation. In ordinary life, the same difficulty extends to judging the ripeness of produce, matching clothing, reading appliance indicator lights, distinguishing wire insulation, and interpreting color-coded medication and safety labels. Occupationally the constraint becomes explicit: in one surveyed sample, 34 percent of respondents with CVD reported that the condition had shaped their career choices, and 24 percent had been barred outright from specific occupations, including transport, policing, and electronics.

Existing responses fall into two broad groups, and neither closes the gap. Corrective and simulative approaches — tinted lenses and display filters — alter how colors appear to the wearer or viewer, but they do not tell the user what a color actually is, and their benefit varies substantially by deficiency type and severity. Identification tools come closer to the practical need, and the use of such applications is a recognized and growing coping strategy among people with CVD. Their weakness is methodological. Most sample pixels from a camera feed and map the values to a name using fixed thresholds in a color space such as RGB or HSL, which ties accuracy directly to illumination: warm indoor lighting, shadow, glare, and reflective or metallic surfaces shift the sampled values and shift the returned label with them. Naming is likewise poorly calibrated, with some tools returning labels too specific to act on and others too coarse to separate the hues users most often confuse.

This gap motivates the present study. A model trained on labelled images, rather than one governed by fixed thresholds, can learn the relationship between what a camera captures and the name a person would assign to that color across the varied lighting conditions in which the tool will actually be used. The researchers therefore developed ChromaSee, a machine-learning model that identifies the dominant color of an object presented to a camera and returns its common name in real time. The system is designed to run on devices the intended users already own, requiring no specialized optics, no dedicated hardware, and no clinical supervision.

The study carries significance for several groups. For individuals with CVD, it offers an immediate and low-cost means of verifying a color independently, without depending on another person's eyes. For teachers and learners, it provides a way to make existing color-dependent instructional materials usable without redesigning them. For workers in trades where color coding carries safety information, it supplies a check that does not rest on color perception. More broadly, the study contributes a locally collected and labelled image dataset and an evaluation of model performance under everyday lighting conditions — a shortcoming that users of existing tools report consistently but that published work has rarely measured.

Inside the modelArchitecture

Four classes, and one of them is "Nothing".

Three colour classes alone would force the model to spread its full confidence across red, yellow and blue even when the desk is empty. An empty desk reading "Red — 100%" is a false positive, and for an assistive tool that is the worst failure available: the learner trusts an answer that was never real.

HUE ≈ 0°

Red

Paper, crayon, cloth, plastic, folder. Red and yellow sit only 60° apart, and that pair is the confusion axis for red–green CVD — the most common type.

HUE ≈ 55°

Yellow

The same five object types in yellow. Sits opposite blue on the tritan axis and close to red on the other, so it borders both confusions.

HUE ≈ 220°

Blue

The same five object types in blue. Maximally distant from yellow on the wheel, which makes it the most stable of the three classes.

DEFAULT STATE

Nothing

Empty desk, bare hand, blank wall, neutral surfaces. Gives the model somewhere legitimate to put its confidence so the interface can stay quiet.

Why a trained model instead of a rule

The obvious approach is a threshold: if the hex value is near #FF0000, say red. It collapses in a real room, because every one of these conditions moves the pixels.

Warm light

A desk lamp or late afternoon shifts the whole frame toward yellow-red. This alone can push a red object's pixels into the yellow range.

Fluorescent ceiling

Shifts the opposite way, toward green-blue, and flattens the separation between classes.

Hand shadow

Brightness drops sharply and the hue reading turns noisy frame to frame.

Gloss and lamination

Specular highlights read as near-white and wash the sample out entirely.

Distance

The object occupies a different share of the frame, so background pixels start to dominate the average.

The fix scales differently

A rule fails and you rewrite conditions. A classifier fails and you show it more of what it got wrong — a repair a teacher can perform without touching code.

How it decidesDeployment logic

A wrong answer stated confidently is worse than no answer.

Every frame returns four confidences. Three guards stand between that and a spoken result, and only a reading that clears all three is ever announced to a learner. The 85% threshold is deliberately conservative.

Model output and the resulting behaviour
Model outputWhat ChromaSee doesState
Top class is Nothing Stays quiet. Shows "Show me an object." Idle
Colour class, 85% or higher Announces. Large colour name, plus speech when enabled. Announce
Colour class, 60–84% "Hold steady — move closer or into better light." No colour is named. Hold
Two classes within 15% of each other "This is not a primary colour." The correct answer for an orange or green object, and logged toward V2. Out of scope

The split rule earns its keep

An orange object has no correct class in a three-colour model. Saying so out loud turns a limitation into honest behaviour instead of a silent error.

One object at a time

Teachable Machine returns a class and a confidence, nothing else. It cannot say where in the frame the colour is, so V1 assumes a single object presented to the camera.

Classroom scale, not district scale

No accounts, no analytics, no server. At district size a coded pipeline would eventually be required, and that is a deliberate boundary rather than an oversight.

The datasetCapture design

75 samples per class, and the variance is designed rather than accidental.

Every class is built from the same matrix: three lighting conditions, five object types, five angles and distances.

Lighting

3
  • Cool — daylight beside the window
  • Neutral — overhead fluorescent or LED
  • Warm — desk lamp or late afternoon

Object type

5
  • Bond paper
  • Crayon or marker
  • Cloth or shirt
  • Plastic item
  • Folder or book cover

Angle · distance

5
  • Flat-on
  • Tilted left
  • Tilted right
  • Close, about 15 cm
  • Far, about 40 cm

3 × 5 × 5 = 75 images per class · 300 in total · roughly 5.4 MB

Warm light is mandatory, not optional

Warm light pushes red pixels toward yellow, and red ↔ yellow is the tightest boundary in this model at only 60° of separation. Training under warm light is what keeps that boundary stable — the model meets its hardest case during training rather than during the demonstration.
